Can café density around a subway station predict where morning crowds concentrate?

00Abstract

This study examines morning subway hotspots in Manhattan. We frame 7–10am ridership surges as a binary classification problem on a spatial graph. A station-day is labeled a hotspot if its morning ridership lies in the top 25% across all observations, corresponding to a threshold of 3,258 riders. Our graph covers 123 Manhattan stations, with edges representing geographic distance and shared route connections.

We train a Simplified Graph Attention Network (ST-GAT) with three feature branches: historical ridership sequences (7-day window), static café density within a 400-meter station radius, and daily weather (temperature, precipitation). After two GATConv layers with a residual skip connection, a classification head outputs per-station hotspot probabilities. The model achieves F1 = 0.91 and AUC = 0.99 on the held-out test set, substantially outperforming LSTM baselines (F1 0.48–0.59).

We introduce the Coffee Contribution Index (CCI), which measures how much each station’s hotspot probability changes when the café input is removed from the model. The CCI reveals a clear geographic pattern where Midtown and lower Manhattan stations score positive, while uptown stations with fewer cafés score negative. This suggests the model uses café density to reflect commercial activity that ridership history alone cannot determine.

01Introduction

During morning rush hour, congestion in certain Manhattan subway stations surges in ways that are difficult to predict. Historical ridership data alone is not enough to predict congestion a day in advance. It requires a model of the neighborhood conditions that determine where and when people travel.

This paper asks whether café density around a subway station can predict morning congestion by signaling neighborhood commercial activity. A station surrounded by 30 cafés within 400 meters attracts foot traffic differently than one with 2, even with identical service.

We test this using a Spatio-Temporal Graph Attention Network trained on 112 days of MTA turnstile data across 123 Manhattan stations. The model takes in three inputs: ridership history, café density, and daily weather, and passes information between stations through a graph built on geographic proximity and shared lines. We then introduce the Coffee Contribution Index (CCI), which measures how much each station’s predicted hotspot probability shifts when the café input is removed.

The results are strong and consistent. The ST-GAT achieves F1 = 0.91 and AUC = 0.99, against LSTM baselines of F1 = 0.48 and 0.59. The CCI shows a geographic split: downtown and Midtown stations carry positive CCI (café density boosts their predicted hotspot probability), while uptown low-café stations show negative CCI (low café count suppresses the prediction). Café density reliably marks the neighborhoods where congestion is most severe.

02Background

Why This Problem Matters

Morning rush hour is one of the most stressful periods for urban transit systems. Knowing where ridership surges are likely to happen can help planners prepare staffing changes, adjust service, and reduce dangerous overcrowding. Current practice relies heavily on historical averages and dispatcher judgment. A model that predicts station-level hotspots 24 hours in advance gives planners time to act before congestion occurs.

The Third-Place Hypothesis

In 1989, urban sociologist Ray Oldenburg defined the “third place” as the informal gathering space that structures daily social life, separate from home and work. Coffee shops are the quintessential third place in cities. They predict foot traffic and clustering where economic activity is high. We hypothesize that café density encodes neighborhood information that ridership history alone does not capture. This includes commercial activity, the mix of office workers and residents, and pre-work gathering habits. Together, these factors improve hotspot prediction.

If the CCI confirms this, hotspots are better understood as an urban behavioral pattern than a transit scheduling problem. Zoning commercial activity around transit nodes may inadvertently concentrate morning demand, straining platform capacity.

Methodological Value

Most ridership forecasting treats each station independently. A spatial graph connecting nearby and shared-line stations lets the model share information across the network. For example, a surge at 72nd St predicts what is coming at 59th St. This reflects how congestion actually moves through the network, making predictions more accurate than per-station LSTM models.

“Coffee does not cause rush hour, but it marks the neighborhoods where rush hour runs deepest.”

03Related Work

Spatio-Temporal Graph Networks for Transit Demand

The standard approach to station-level forecasting combines graph neural networks with recurrent architectures, using graphs built from physical connections or passenger flow patterns [1]. Recent models pair graph attention layers with GRU and dilated convolutions [2]. We simplify this by representing time as a flat 7-day window rather than a recurrent state, given our limited training data.

Some work questions whether graph structure always improves forecasting, arguing that GCN-based models struggle to capture global spatial patterns [3]. Our LSTM baseline directly tests this, isolating how much of the ST-GAT’s performance gain comes from spatial message passing versus the richer feature set.

Incorporating External Features: POI, Weather, Land Use

Point-of-Interest (POI) data is widely used to capture the functional character of station neighborhoods, and weather is a standard travel influence [4]. Some models combine land use, sociodemographics, and POI distributions through more complex attention mechanisms [5]. Our café density branch is a more focused version of this, isolating third-place density as a behavioral signal rather than treating all nearby amenities as equivalent. We are not aware of prior work that introduces a dedicated attribution index for a single POI category.

Text and Event-Based Context

Natural language signals in transit forecasting are less common but have precedent. Early work applied topic modeling to event descriptions alongside smart card data [6], and later work used bidirectional LSTMs combining tweets with traffic and weather to handle non-recurring events [7]. We originally planned a fourth branch encoding daily Manhattan news headlines via Sentence-BERT, but dropped it after finding that text embeddings added variance without reducing loss over our 112-day training window. This is consistent with the view that text signals are most useful for discrete events rather than background neighborhood context.

Urban Hotspot Detection

Most hotspot detection research retrospectively clusters mobility data [8]. Treating hotspot status as a classification target for a predictive model is less common in North American transit, but more useful in practice since it informs the decisions made before congestion occurs.

What This Work Adds

How commercial POI density contributes to morning surge classification in a North American subway network, and whether that contribution can be separated from ridership and weather signals at the station level, has not been directly examined. The CCI addresses this and has no direct equivalent in the literature we reviewed.

Hotspot Label

A station-day is labeled a hotspot if its morning ridership is at or above the 75th percentile of all station-day ridership values in the dataset. That threshold is 3,258 riders for the 7–10am window. This produces a binary label (1 = hotspot, 0 = not), with about 25% of station-days positive. We apply a global threshold rather than a per-station one because we want the model to learn an absolute standard of congestion. By this standard, a quiet uptown station that has an unusually busy morning is not a hotspot.

Station Graph

The graph connects two stations if their geodesic distance is under one kilometer or they share at least one subway route. Edge weights decay with distance: wij = exp(−α dij) where α is fit to place the median weight at 0.5. This yields a graph with connectivity ranging from 2 edges (TRAM2) to 69 edges (59 St–Columbus Circle).

05ML Formulation

Let G = (V, E) be the station graph with |V| = 123 nodes. For each station v ∈ V and day t, the model receives three input streams:

xride(v, t)
7-day morning ridership window ending at t−1, shaped (7,) per station. Scaled with a StandardScaler fitted on the training split.
xcafé(v)
Static café count within 400m of station v, computed once via BallTree haversine query. Scaled independently.
xwx(t)
Daily maximum temperature and precipitation (2-dim), broadcast to all stations on day t. Fitted on training days only.

Output: ŷv,t ∈ [0,1], the predicted probability that station v is a hotspot on day t. Labels are binary. Objective: BCEWithLogitsLoss with pos_weight = 3.0 to compensate for the 3:1 class imbalance. We use reduction="none" and mask out station-days with missing ridership data before computing the mean loss.

ℒ=− 1|𝓜| ∑(v,t)∈𝓜 [ yv,tlogy^v,t + (1−yv,t) log(1−y^v,t) ]

where 𝓜 is the set of valid station-day observations. Primary evaluation metrics are F1 score (handles class imbalance; a model predicting “never hotspot” scores F1 = 0, not 75% accuracy) and AUC-ROC (threshold-independent). The data is split chronologically: train = days 1–87, validation = days 88–97, test = days 98–112. All scalers are fitted on training data only.

06Model Architecture

The model is a three-branch Graph Attention Network we call SimplifiedGAT. The “simplified” refers to the deliberate removal of an outer temporal GRU: with only 87 training days, a recurrent layer over the day dimension risked fitting noise rather than structure. Instead, the 7-day ridership window enters as a flat vector, treated as a multi-feature input rather than a sequence.

Branch Encoding

Each branch maps its input to a 64-dimensional station-level embedding. The ridership branch applies Linear(7→64) + ReLU to the 7-day window for each station. The café branch applies Linear(1→64) + ReLU to the scalar café count (the same static value is used across all days for a given station). The weather branch applies Linear(2→64) + ReLU to the two-dimensional weather vector, then broadcasts it to all 123 nodes.

Fusion and Graph Attention

The three 64-dim embeddings are concatenated into a 192-dim vector per station, then compressed by Linear(192→64) + ReLU into a shared station embedding. This embedding enters the graph attention stack.

GAT layer 1 is GATConv(64→64, heads=4, concat=True), producing a 256-dimensional output by concatenating the 4 attention heads. GAT layer 2 is GATConv(256→64, heads=4, concat=False), averaging the heads back to 64 dimensions. After the second layer, we add a residual connection from the fusion output: x = ReLU(x_gat2 + x_fused). Without this skip connection, the graph attention mechanism collapsed all 123 node representations toward the same embedding, causing the model to predict near-constant probabilities and achieve AUC near 0.5. The residual connection preserves each station’s individual information across the two-hop neighborhood aggregation.

Classification Head and Training

A Dropout(0.3) layer followed by Linear(64→1) produces one logit per station. At inference, sigmoid maps each logit to a probability in [0,1]; the classification threshold is 0.5. Total parameters: 96,513.

Optimizer: AdamW with lr = 1e−2 and weight_decay = 1e−3. Learning rate scheduler: ReduceLROnPlateau (mode = max, factor = 0.5, patience = 5) monitoring validation AUC. The model converged at epoch 7 with validation F1 = 0.9386.

Baselines

Two LSTM baselines treat each station as an independent time series, with no graph structure. Model 0 uses ridership only (3-day window). Model 1 appends static café density to the LSTM input (7-day window). Comparing Model 0 to Model 1 isolates the café signal without spatial reasoning; comparing Model 1 to ST-GAT isolates the contribution of graph attention.

Reading the Numbers

The results split into two clear gaps. The first, from LSTM-0 to LSTM-1, comes from a single static scalar: café count within 400 meters, adding 11 F1 points and 8 AUC points. If that scalar were just a noisy proxy for ridership, it would add no signal to a model already trained on ridership history. The fact that it does means the model is picking up something about neighborhood character that the ridership time series does not contain.

The second gap, from LSTM-1 to ST-GAT (+32 F1 points, +20 AUC), isolates the value of spatial message passing. The graph attention layers allow the model to condition each station’s prediction on the recent state of its neighbors. A surge developing one stop away is informative about what is about to happen at the next station. This mirrors how congestion actually propagates through the network, which is why the structural improvement is so large.

The confusion matrix (1,301 true negatives, 23 false positives, 70 false negatives, 449 true positives) shows the model is precision-dominant. It rarely flags a quiet station as a hotspot, but misses about 13% of actual hotspots. In practice, this means unexpected surges occasionally go unpredicted, though false alarms are rare.

Was AI Really Needed?

A threshold rule on yesterday’s ridership achieves roughly 70% accuracy but struggles on days that diverge from recent patterns, such as those with atypical weather, seasonal effects, or irregular workplace attendance. The graph attention layer handles these cases by drawing on correlated patterns from neighboring stations to catch what local history misses.

08Coffee Contribution Index

While the ST-GAT predicts hotspot probabilities well, the deeper question is: how much does the café branch actually contribute to those predictions, and does it differ across the city in a way that makes geographic sense? The Coffee Contribution Index (CCI) is designed to answer this.

Computation

CCI is a zero-ablation attribution measure. For each test day t and each station v, we run the trained model twice: once with the full input, and once with the café branch input set to zero before it passes through the encoder. The difference in output probabilities, averaged over the 15 test days, gives a signed contribution score:

CCIraw(v) = 1|Ttest| ∑t∈Ttest [ σ(f(xv,t)) − σ(f(xv,t|xcafé→0)) ]

A positive CCI means the café branch raises the hotspot probability, such that removing it causes the model to predict a lower chance of a surge. This is the pattern at high-café downtown and Midtown stations, where the model relies on café density as a signal of neighborhood commercial intensity.

A negative CCI means that removing the café branch raises the predicted probability, as the model expects a low-café station to be less active and removing that suppressive signal causes it to over-predict. This appears at uptown stations where café density is genuinely low. The signed measure captures both effects in a single chart rather than treating low-CCI stations as simply unaffected.

We evaluated three attribution methods during development: zero-ablation (final choice), mean-ablation (setting the café input to its training-set mean rather than zero), and gradient sensitivity (dΣp / d x_café). Mean-ablation and zero-ablation produced nearly identical results because StandardScaler maps the training mean to zero, making the encoded representation under either ablation essentially the same. Gradient sensitivity produced higher variance estimates but identified uptown stations as most sensitive, which is counterintuitive given their low café counts, since gradient magnitude reflects model uncertainty rather than directional contribution. Zero-ablation is preferred because the sign is directly meaningful and the magnitude represents a verifiable probability shift.

Geographic Pattern

The results are geographically coherent. The highest positive CCI stations cluster in Midtown and lower Manhattan: 57 St–7 Av (CCI = +0.036), 47–50 Sts Rockefeller Center, 34 St Herald Square. These neighborhoods have dense café clusters. 57 St–7 Av has roughly 60 coffee shops within 400 meters, and morning activity that ridership history alone underpredicts. The model uses high café density as evidence of commercial morning activity, raising hotspot probability.

The largest negative CCI values appear at uptown stations: 125 St A,C,B,D (CCI = −0.133), 145 St, 163 St–Amsterdam Av. These stations have 2–4 cafés within radius. The model has learned to associate low café counts with lower activity, so it applies downward pressure on hotspot probability at these stations. Removing the café input loosens that suppression, causing the model to over-predict.

This asymmetry matters for interpretation. The CCI is directional, not just a measure of attention. Downtown stations benefit from high café counts while uptown stations are penalized by low ones. The CCI alone cannot distinguish whether this reflects real behavioral differences or simply tracks income and employment patterns; however, it can be tested in future work.

09Limitations & Future Work

The most significant constraint is the training window. The 112-day window covers January through late April 2026, omitting possible fluctuations in ridership due to summer vacation, back-to-school season, and winter holidays. A model trained only on these months may have learned seasonal patterns specific to that period. Extending to a full calendar year would reduce this risk. The high AUC (0.99) should also be interpreted with caution: with only 15 test days and highly autocorrelated labels—the same Midtown stations tend to be hotspots day after day—the test set may not expose the model to enough distributional shift to reveal overfitting. A longer, seasonally diverse evaluation window would provide a more demanding test.

Second, café density is treated as static throughout training and evaluation, based on counts from the NYC restaurant inspection database. In practice, cafés open and close, and some database entries may be outdated. A live feed from Yelp or Google Places would make the café signal dynamic, and could capture how ridership patterns shift when a new café opens near a previously low-density station.

Third, the CCI measures model sensitivity, not behavioral causality. A positive CCI does not imply that adding cafés near a station would increase ridership. The attribution reflects patterns learned from a distribution confounded by neighborhood characteristics like income, employment type, and residential density. Separating causation from correlation would require a natural experiment allowing a before and after comparison.

Given three more months, we would pursue: (1) a full calendar year of training data to capture seasonality; (2) dynamic café density updated weekly from live business data; (3) a multi-city replication test in Chicago or Washington D.C. to examine whether the café-hotspot relationship generalizes beyond the specific commercial geography of Manhattan; and (4) a longitudinal study tracking changes in CCI scores as the restaurant landscape in specific neighborhoods evolves.

10Ethics

This project uses publicly available, aggregate data: MTA ridership tallied by station and day, restaurant registration records, and weather observations. No individual trips are tracked or reconstructed. Predictions are station-level probability scores, which do not enable re-identification of individual movements.

The equity implications must also be addressed. The model learns from data that reflects decades of unequal urban investment. Uptown stations have lower café density because commercial investment in those neighborhoods has historically been lower. A negative CCI at these stations should not be misinterpreted as a reason to reduce service in these areas.

Commercial use of the CCI raises a related concern. A business using high-CCI stations to identify prime café locations could contribute to displacement in already transitioning neighborhoods. Similarly, using CCI to guide capital investment could direct resources toward already well-served Midtown stations at the expense of others.

Deployment for planning decisions should include equity audits and input from affected communities. The model is a tool; its implications depend on how it is used.
