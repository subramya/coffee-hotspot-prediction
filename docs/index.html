Applied Machine Learning

Morning Brews
& Morning Rush

Learning coffee-driven subway hotspots in Manhattan

00Abstract

We investigate morning subway “hotspots” in Manhattan by modeling 7–10am station-level ridership surges as a spatio-temporal representation learning problem. Restricting our analysis to Manhattan subway stations, we construct a spatial graph where nodes represent stations and edges encode geographic proximity and shared line connectivity.

We train a Spatio-Temporal Graph Attention Network (GAT) combined with a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) to learn latent station embeddings that capture morning demand dynamics. The model uses a multi-branch neural architecture to separately encode (1) historical ridership patterns, (2) station-level café and third-place density, (3) weather variables, and (4) daily urban context embeddings generated from Manhattan news headlines via Sentence-BERT.

We introduce a Coffee Contribution Index (CCI) computed through structured feature perturbation and branch-level attribution to quantify how strongly third-place density drives hotspot formation across neighborhoods. We benchmark against a sequence-only LSTM that excludes graph structure, isolating the added value of spatial message passing.

02Background

Why This Problem Matters

Morning rush hour is the single most stressful period for transit systems. Identifying where surges will concentrate—not just that they will happen—gives city planners and the MTA actionable intelligence for deploying staff, adjusting service frequency, or redesigning platform flow before congestion becomes a safety issue.

The Third-Place Hypothesis

The inclusion of café and third-place density is the intellectually interesting bet in this research. The implicit hypothesis is that subway hotspots are not purely commuter-driven; they are also shaped by where people choose to linger before work. If the Coffee Contribution Index confirms this, it reframes hotspots as a behavioral and urban design phenomenon rather than just a transit scheduling problem—with real implications for how cities zone commercial activity near transit and how businesses choose locations.

Methodological Value

Most ridership forecasting treats stations as independent time series. Building a spatial graph that encodes proximity and shared line connectivity lets the model propagate information between stations: a surge at 72nd St likely signals what is coming at 59th St. This mirrors how congestion actually moves through a network, making predictions structurally more honest.

The multi-branch architecture with structured feature perturbation is designed to answer why a hotspot forms, not just whether it will—a harder and more valuable question, since policymakers need causal levers, not just forecasts.

"The core reason this problem matters is that it connects transit operations, urban behavioral economics, and causal machine learning in a way that could directly inform how dense cities manage their most congested daily moments."

03Related Work

Spatio-Temporal Graph Networks for Transit Demand

The dominant model in this area encodes transit stations as graph nodes and uses graph neural networks to capture spatial dependencies alongside temporal models. Researchers have constructed graphs from physical connections between stations, passenger flow sequence similarities, and functional correlations to learn effective spatio-temporal representations [1]. The combination of GATs with recurrent architectures like GRU is well-established; one prominent approach uses dilated convolutions to extract multi-dimensional temporal dependencies, then applies GRU on metro ridership data from two Chinese cities [2].

There has also been pushback on whether graph structure always helps. Some work argues that GCN-based models fail to capture global spatial correlations effectively, motivating hybrid architectures [3]. The LSTM-only baseline in this paper directly engages with that question, isolating the contribution of spatial message passing to hotspot prediction.

Incorporating External Features: POI, Weather, Land Use

Point-of-Interest (POI) data is widely used to capture the distribution of functional areas around stations; weather is a standard travel influence [4]. Work like ST-MFGCRN fuses land use, sociodemographics, and POI distribution through a sentinel attention mechanism [5]. The café density branch in this model is a more specific and theoretically motivated version of the POI signal: rather than treating all nearby amenities as equivalent, it isolates third-place density as a behavioral driver and tests that hypothesis through structured attribution.

Text and Event-Based Signals

Using natural language data in transit prediction is less common but has real precedent. Earlier approaches applied topic modeling to event descriptions with transit smart card data [6]. One study proposed a bi-directional LSTM combining tweet data with traffic and weather, specifically to handle non-recurring events that degrade accuracy in purely data-driven models [7]. Our use of Sentence-BERT on daily Manhattan news headlines extends this from event-specific triggers toward ambient urban context—an input type that remains largely unexplored.

Urban Hotspot Detection

The broader hotspot detection literature mostly focuses on clustering mobility or trajectory data retrospectively [8]. Framing hotspot status as a classification target for a neural model moves the problem from retrospective identification to forward-looking prediction—the operationally useful formulation for transit management.

What This Work Adds

Prior work has established strong baselines for spatio-temporal graph prediction and multi-feature fusion, but has not combined these tools in the specific context of morning surge classification in a dense North American subway network, nor attempted to disentangle the contribution of third-place commercial density from commuter and environmental signals. The Coffee Contribution Index does not have a close analog in the existing literature.

05ML Formulation

Let G = (V, E) be the station graph with |V| = 123 nodes. For each station v ∈ V and day t, the model receives four input streams:

xride
7-day historical ridership sequence (T × 1)
xcafé
Static café density within 400m (scalar)
xwx
Daily temperature and precipitation (2-dim)
xnews
Sentence-BERT headline embedding, PCA-reduced to dim d

Output: ŷv,t ∈ [0,1], the predicted probability that station v is a hotspot on day t. Labels are binary (y = 1 if top-25% ridership, else 0). Objective: binary cross-entropy.

ℒ=− 1N ∑i=1N [ yilogy^i + (1−yi) log(1−y^i) ]

Primary evaluation: F1 score (handles class imbalance; 75% negative labels) and AUC-ROC (threshold-independent). Accuracy is not used as a primary metric—a model predicting “never hotspot” achieves 75% accuracy and is useless.
